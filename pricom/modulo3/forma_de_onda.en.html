Waveform and PCM
A waveform coder copies the signal sample by sample. PCM quantizes each sample on its own.

Copying the waveform

PCM, DPCM and DM on the same samples · press → to advance
A waveform coder tries to reproduce at the destination the same waveform as the source, sample by sample, with the smallest possible distortion. It does not look at the mechanism that generated the signal, only at properties of the waveform. That is why it works for very different sources.
PCM: each sample is quantized and encoded on its own. The encoder sends the full height of each blue bar.
DPCM: neighboring samples are similar, so it sends only the jump from one to the next, the red arrows. The jumps are much smaller than the heights and fit in fewer bits.
DM: the extreme case of DPCM, with sampling so fast that the jump is encoded with a single bit, up or down.

PCM

Filter, sampler, quantizer and encoder · Fig. 7.7 of Proakis
PCM, or pulse code modulation, is the simplest and oldest waveform scheme. It is the three stages of A/D conversion in sequence, Fig. 7.7 of Proakis.
Assumption 1: bandwidth limited to \(W\). The presampling filter removes what lies above \(W\); the gray curve becomes the black one.
Sampling with \(f_s \ge 2W\), usually a little above, to leave a guard band.
Assumption 2: limited amplitude, \(|x(t)| \le x_{\max}\). Assumption 3: many levels, \(N = 2^\nu\). Each sample goes to the nearest level.
Each level becomes a word of \(\nu\) bits. The rate is \(R = \nu f_s\) bits/s.

Uniform PCM

Distortion and SQNR · press → to advance
Range \([-x_{\max}, x_{\max}]\) in \(N = 2^\nu\) equal regions
\[ \Delta = \frac{2x_{\max}}{N} = \frac{2x_{\max}}{2^\nu} \tag{1.1}\]
Uniform error within the step
\[ D = \cssId{pu-d12}{\frac{\Delta^2}{12}} = \frac{4x_{\max}^2}{12\cdot 4^\nu} = \frac{x_{\max}^2}{3\cdot 4^\nu} \tag{1.2}\]
Signal power over distortion
\[ \text{SQNR} = \frac{P_X}{D} = 3\cdot 4^\nu\,\frac{\cssId{pu-px}{P_X}}{x_{\max}^2} \tag{1.3}\]
Limited amplitude
\[ |X| \le x_{\max}\ \Rightarrow\ \cssId{pu-px4}{P_X} = E[X^2] \le x_{\max}^2 \]
Substitute into (1.3)
\[ \text{SQNR} \le \cssId{pu-teto}{3\cdot 4^\nu} \]
In dB, \(10\log_{10}\) of (1.3)
\[ \text{SQNR}_{\text{dB}} \approx 10\log_{10}\frac{P_X}{x_{\max}^2} + \cssId{pu-6}{6\nu} + \cssId{pu-48}{4.8} \tag{1.4}\]
The larger the dynamic range of the source, the smaller \(P_X/x_{\max}^2\) and the worse the SQNR.
\(x_{\max},\ \nu\)
amplitude limit; bits per sample
\(D\)
distortion, mean power of the error \(\hat x - x\)
\(P_X\)
signal power, \(E[X^2]\)
uniform error in \((-\Delta/2, \Delta/2]\)
\(|X| \le x_{\max}\), so \(P_X \le x_{\max}^2\)
ceiling: only with the signal filling the whole range
\(10\log_{10}4^\nu = \nu\cdot 6.02\)
\(10\log_{10}3 = 4.77\)

Example: uniform signal

Worked on the board · press → to advance
Signal uniform on \([-1, 1]\), uniform PCM with \(N = 256\) levels. What is the SQNR?
Power from the density
\[ P_X = \int_{-1}^{1} x^2\, \cssId{eu-fx}{\tfrac{1}{2}}\, dx \]
Antiderivative of \(x^2\)
\[ P_X = \frac12 \cssId{eu-prim}{\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{1}} \]
Evaluate
\[ P_X = \frac12\cdot\frac23 = \frac13 \]
Substitute into (1.3)
\[ \text{SQNR} = 3\cdot 4^{\cssId{eu-nu}{8}}\cdot\frac{1/3}{\cssId{eu-xm}{1}^2} \]
The 3 cancels
\[ \text{SQNR} = 4^8 = 65\,536 \]
In dB
\[ 10\log_{10} 65\,536 = 80\log_{10}2 \approx \boxed{48.16 \text{ dB}} \]
All levels are equally used and the error is exactly uniform. This is the case in which uniform PCM reaches the ceiling: 6 dB per bit, starting from 0 dB.
area 1 over width 2
\(\frac13 - \left(-\frac13\right) = \frac23\)
\(\nu = \log_2 256\)
\(x_{\max} = 1\)

PCM bandwidth

How much spectrum the bits cost · press → to advance
\(\nu\) bits per sample, \(f_s\) samples per second
\[ R = \nu\, f_s \;\text{bits/s} \tag{1.5}\]
\(R\) bits/s occupy at least \(R/2\) Hz, chapter 10
\[ \text{BW} = \frac{\nu\, \cssId{bw-fs}{f_s}}{2} \]
At the Nyquist rate
\[ \text{BW} = \frac{\nu\cdot \cssId{bw-2w}{2W}}{2} = \boxed{\nu W} \tag{1.6}\]
PCM multiplies the signal bandwidth by at least \(\nu\).
Speech with \(W = 4\) kHz and \(\nu = 8\): the analog signal occupies 4 kHz and PCM at least 32 kHz.
In return, the bits get through the noise and can be regenerated at each repeater.
\(f_s = 2W\), Nyquist rate

Dynamic range

The SQNR of uniform PCM depends on the loudness
The SQNR of uniform PCM drops 1 dB for every dB less of power.
A loud speaker uses the whole range.
A soft speaker is 30 dB worse.
In speech, small amplitudes are the most likely and fall into the few levels in the center.
We want an SQNR independent of loudness: small steps near zero, large ones at the edges.

Nonuniform PCM
A compressor before the uniform quantizer and an expander after it: small steps for small amplitudes.

Companding

Compressor, uniform PCM and expander · Fig. 7.8 of Proakis
Fig. 7.8 of Proakis: four blocks between the sample \(x_n\) and the reconstruction \(\hat{x}_n\).
The compressor reduces the dynamic range before quantization.
The quantizer is still uniform, but it works on \(g(x)\).
At the receiver, the expander applies the inverse: compressing plus expanding, companding.
The whole is a nonuniform quantizer in \(x\). The name nonlinear quantization is misleading: every quantizer is nonlinear.

Compressor on the board

A small and a large sample passing through the curve · press → to advance
Logarithmic compressor, here with \(\mu = 20\) to see the curve, and only the positive side: \[ g(x) = \frac{\ln(1 + 20x)}{\ln 21} \] Large slope near zero and small slope near 1.
The uniform quantizer splits the vertical axis into 8 equal bands, with the level in the middle of each one: \[ g = \tfrac{k}{8},\quad \text{levels } \tfrac{2k+1}{16} \]
Projecting the boundaries through the curve down to the \(x\) axis: narrow regions near zero and wide ones near 1. \[ x_k = g^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{k}{8}\right) = \frac{21^{k/8} - 1}{20} \] The first region has width 0.023 and the last 0.33.
Small sample, in green: \[ \begin{aligned} x &= 0.05 \;\to\; g(x) = 0.228\\ &\to\; \text{level } 0.1875\\ &\to\; \hat{x} = g^{-1}(0.1875) = 0.039 \end{aligned} \] Error of 0.011.
Large sample, in red: \[ \begin{aligned} x &= 0.7 \;\to\; g(x) = 0.890\\ &\to\; \text{level } 0.9375 \;\to\; \hat{x} = 0.818 \end{aligned} \] Error of 0.118: ten times larger, but the sample is 14 times larger. The error follows the amplitude, and the signal-to-error ratio is similar for both.

μ-law

Compressor of the USA, Canada and Japan · press → to advance
Compressor with \(|x| \le 1\)
\[ g(x) = \frac{\ln(1 + \mu|x|)}{\ln(1 + \mu)}\,\cssId{mu-sg}{\operatorname{sgn}(x)} \tag{2.1}\]
\(\mu \to 0\): \(\ln(1+u) \approx u\) for small \(u\)
\[ g(x) \approx \frac{\cssId{mu-num}{\mu|x|}}{\cssId{mu-den}{\mu}}\,\operatorname{sgn}(x) = x \]
No compression at all: uniform PCM. With \(\mu = 5\) the curve already rises faster near zero.
The standard is \(\mu = 255\) with \(\nu = 8\) bits per sample. The gain over uniform PCM reaches about 24 dB at low amplitudes.
odd curve: the sign passes straight through
\(\ln(1+\mu|x|) \approx \mu|x|\) and \(\ln(1+\mu) \approx \mu\)

A-law

Compressor of Europe and Brazil · press → to advance
Straight line near zero, logarithm elsewhere
\[ g(x) = \operatorname{sgn}(x)\begin{cases} \dfrac{A|x|}{1 + \ln A}, & |x| < \frac{1}{A}\\[3mm] \dfrac{1 + \ln(A|x|)}{1 + \ln A}, & \frac{1}{A} \le |x| \le 1 \end{cases} \tag{2.2}\]
Near zero the logarithm would go to \(-\infty\): the A-law uses a straight line there.
The two parts meet at \(|x| = 1/A\)
\[ \frac{A\cdot\frac1A}{1+\ln A} = \frac{1}{1+\ln A} \qquad \cssId{la-l1}{\frac{1 + \ln 1}{1 + \ln A}} = \frac{1}{1+\ln A} \]
The standard uses \(A = 87.6\), with performance similar to that of the μ-law with \(\mu = 255\).
\(\ln 1 = 0\)

Why the logarithm

Which curve makes the SQNR independent of loudness · press → to advance
What shape must \(g(x)\) have for the SQNR not to depend on loudness?
The step \(\Delta\) on the vertical axis becomes a step \(\Delta_x\) on the \(x\) axis.
Steep curve, narrow \(\Delta_x\); flat curve, wide \(\Delta_x\)
\[ \Delta \approx \cssId{pl-gl}{g'(x)}\,\Delta_x \ \Longrightarrow\ \Delta_x \approx \frac{\Delta}{g'(x)} \tag{2.3}\]
Uniform error in the region of width \(\Delta_x\)
\[ D(x) \approx \frac{\Delta_x^2}{12} = \frac{\Delta^2}{12\,g'(x)^2} \tag{2.4}\]
\(x^2/D(x)\) constant
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{x^2}{D(x)} &= \frac{12}{\Delta^2}\,\cssId{pl-xg}{x^2 g'(x)^2}\\ &\Longrightarrow\ g'(x) \propto \frac{1}{|x|} \ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{g(x) \propto \ln |x|} \end{aligned} \]
tangent line within the step
constant only if \(|x|\,g'(x)\) is constant

SQNR of the μ-law

Derivation · press → to advance
Differentiate (2.1) for \(x > 0\)
\[ g'(x) = \frac{1}{\ln(1+\mu)}\cdot\cssId{sm-dl}{\frac{\mu}{1 + \mu x}} \]
\(\mu x \gg 1\)
\[ g'(x) \approx \frac{1}{\ln(1+\mu)}\cdot\frac{1}{\cssId{sm-xx}{x}} \]
Substitute into (2.3) and (2.4)
\[ \Delta_x \approx \Delta\,\ln(1+\mu)\,|x| \ \Rightarrow\ D \approx \frac{\Delta^2 \ln^2(1+\mu)}{12}\,\cssId{sm-ex}{E[X^2]} \]
Divide \(E[X^2]\) by \(D\)
\[ \text{SQNR} = \frac{E[X^2]}{D} \approx \frac{12}{\cssId{sm-dd}{\Delta^2}\ln^2(1+\mu)} \]
\(\Delta = 2/2^\nu\)
\[ \text{SQNR} \approx \frac{\cssId{sm-34}{3\cdot 4^\nu}}{\ln^2(1+\mu)} \tag{2.5}\]
\(\nu = 8\), \(\mu = 255\)
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{SQNR}_{\text{dB}} &\approx 4.77 + 48.16 - 20\log_{10}\ln 256\\ &= 52.93 - 14.88 = \boxed{38.05 \text{ dB}} \end{aligned} \]
chain rule on \(\ln(1 + \mu x)\)
\(\mu x \gg 1\): with \(\mu = 255\), \(x\) above about 0.02
mean of \(x^2\) over all samples
\(E[X^2]\) canceled: it does not depend on loudness
\(\Delta^2 = 4/4^\nu\), so \(12/\Delta^2 = 3\cdot 4^\nu\)

μ-law versus uniform

Gaussian source, \(\nu = 8\) bits, \(x_{\max} = 1\) · press → to advance
Gaussian source with variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\nu = 8\) bits and \(x_{\max} = 1\), ignoring overload. The uniform quantizer follows (1.4), a straight line of slope 1.
The μ-law stays near 38 dB over a range of more than 40 dB of loudness. It only drops when the signal is so weak that \(\mu|x| \gg 1\) no longer holds.
At \(\sigma^2 = -40\) dB, the soft speech of the dynamic range slide, the gain is 22.6 dB.
The price: at maximum loudness the uniform quantizer is better. The μ-law trades the SQNR of strong signals for the same SQNR for all.

DPCM
Neighboring samples are similar: quantizing the difference to the previous reconstruction takes fewer bits.

Neighboring samples

The difference has a smaller range than the signal · press → to advance
PCM quantizes each sample on its own, as if the previous one said nothing about the next.
But a band-limited signal sampled at the Nyquist rate or above changes little from one sample to the next.
The difference between neighbors carries the same information, because \(x_n = x_{n-1} + (x_n - x_{n-1})\), and it is much smaller.
Smaller range: with the same \(\Delta\) fewer levels are enough, hence fewer bits. This is the idea of DPCM.

Power of the difference

Stationary process with power \(\sigma^2\), sampled every \(T_s\)
Expand the square
\[ E\big[(X_n - X_{n-1})^2\big] = \cssId{pd-a1}{E[X_n^2]} - 2\,\cssId{pd-a2}{E[X_n X_{n-1}]} + \cssId{pd-a3}{E[X_{n-1}^2]} \]
Stationary
\[ E\big[(X_n - X_{n-1})^2\big] = \cssId{pd-b1}{2\sigma^2} - \cssId{pd-b2}{2R_X(T_s)} \]
Define \(\rho\)
\[ E\big[(X_n - X_{n-1})^2\big] = \boxed{2\sigma^2(1 - \cssId{pd-rho}{\rho})} \tag{3.1}\]
Similar samples, \(\rho = 0.95\)
\[ 2\sigma^2(1 - 0.95) = 0.1\,\sigma^2 \ \to\ \cssId{pd-10}{10\ \text{dB}} \]
Flat spectrum on \(|f| < W\), \(f_s = 2W\)
\[ R_X(T_s) = \sigma^2\,\operatorname{sinc}(2W T_s) = \cssId{pd-s1}{\sigma^2\operatorname{sinc}(1) = 0} \]
\(E[X_n^2] = E[X_{n-1}^2] = \sigma^2\)
\(R_X(T_s)\), autocorrelation at lag \(T_s\)
\(\rho = R_X(T_s)/\sigma^2\)
10 dB less, or \(10/6.02 \approx 1.7\) bit less per sample
\(\rho = 0\): the difference has twice the power and DPCM does not help

A plain difference accumulates error

Quantizing \(x_n - x_{n-1}\) is not enough · press → to advance
First attempt: quantize \(d_n = x_n - x_{n-1}\) with error \(q_n\) and add up at the receiver.
Quantized difference received
\[ \hat{x}_n = \hat{x}_{n-1} + \cssId{ac-dq}{d_n + q_n} \]
Subtract \(x_n = x_{n-1} + d_n\)
\[ \hat{x}_n - x_n = (\hat{x}_{n-1} - x_{n-1}) + q_n \]
Unroll the recursion
\[ \hat{x}_n - x_n = \cssId{ac-soma}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i} \tag{3.2}\]
With \(\Delta = 0.1\), after 60 samples the error already exceeds six steps.
The DPCM fix: the transmitter computes the difference to the reconstruction \(\hat{x}_{n-1}\) that the receiver has, not to \(x_{n-1}\). In green, the error stays within \(\pm\Delta/2 = \pm 0.05\).
quantized difference received
each error stays forever and the next one adds on top

DPCM

Encoder and decoder · Fig. 7.11 of Proakis
Fig. 7.11 of Proakis. The quantizer receives \(y_n = x_n - \hat{y}'_{n-1}\), and not \(x_n - x_{n-1}\).
Forward path: subtract the prediction and quantize the remainder. Only \(\hat{y}_n\) goes to the channel.
Feedback loop: accumulates the \(\hat{y}_n\) already sent, \(\hat{y}'_n = \hat{y}_n + \hat{y}'_{n-1}\). It is a copy of the receiver inside the transmitter.
The decoder does the same computation: \(\hat{x}_n = \hat{y}_n + \hat{x}_{n-1}\). Same equation, same input.
So \(\hat{y}'_n\) is exactly what the receiver reconstructs, and the transmitter subtracts the reconstruction, as at the end of the previous slide. The numbers in the diagram are from sample \(n = 1\) of the step-by-step example.

DPCM error

Derivation · press → to advance
Encoder
\[ y_n = x_n - \hat{y}'_{n-1} \qquad \cssId{ed-11}{\hat{y}'_n = \hat{y}_n + \hat{y}'_{n-1}} \tag{7.4.11}\]
Decoder
\[ \cssId{ed-13}{\hat{x}_n = \hat{y}_n + \hat{x}_{n-1}} \tag{7.4.13}\]
The two recursions have the same form.
Same input, same start
\[ \hat{x}_n = \hat{y}'_n \quad\text{for all } n \]
Quantizer error
\[ \hat{y}_n - y_n = \hat{y}_n - (x_n - \hat{y}'_{n-1}) \]
Group \(\hat{y}_n + \hat{y}'_{n-1}\)
\[ \hat{y}_n - y_n = \cssId{ed-ys}{\hat{y}'_n} - x_n \]
Use \(\hat{x}_n = \hat{y}'_n\)
\[ \hat{y}_n - y_n = \boxed{\hat{x}_n - x_n} \]
The reconstruction error is the error of a single quantizer step. It does not accumulate, and the range to quantize is that of \(y_n\), much smaller than that of \(x_n\).
same recursion with the same input \(\hat{y}_n\), both starting from 0
\(\hat{y}_n + \hat{y}'_{n-1}\) together

Delta modulation

One-bit quantizer and accumulator · press → to advance
DM is DPCM with the simplest possible quantizer.
1-bit quantizer: the output is \(\hat{y}_n = \Delta e_n\), with \(e_n = \pm 1\).
The bit is \(e_n = \operatorname{sgn}(x_n - \hat{x}_{n-1})\): the staircase goes up or down by \(\Delta\).
At the receiver
\[ \hat{x}_n - \hat{x}_{n-1} = \hat{y}_n \tag{7.4.15}\]
Unroll the recursion with \(\hat{x}_{-1} = 0\)
\[ \hat{x}_n = \hat{y}_n + \hat{y}_{n-1} + \hat{x}_{n-2} = \cdots = \cssId{dm-acc}{\sum_{i=0}^{n} \hat{y}_i} \tag{4.1}\]
With one bit, the difference must be very small: nearly equal samples, \(\rho \approx 1\). That is why DM samples far above the Nyquist rate.
accumulator; with impulses, an integrator

Overload limit

Slope of the staircase versus slope of the signal · press → to advance
The staircase rises at most \(\Delta\) every \(T_s\)
\[ \text{maximum slope of the staircase} = \frac{\Delta}{T_s} = \Delta f_s \]
Sinusoid \(x(t) = A\sin(2\pi f_m t)\)

A weak signal and then a strong one · press → to advance
Adaptation rule, Eq. 7.4.17, with \(K = 1.5\)
\[ \Delta_n = \Delta_{n-1}\, K^{\cssId{ad-ee}{e_n e_{n-1}}} \tag{7.4.17}\]
A weak signal and then a strong one, like speech that changes loudness. No fixed \(\Delta\) serves both segments. Fixed \(\Delta = 0.025\): 7.4 dB in the weak segment, but overload in the strong one, 0.8 dB.

Adaptive: 12.5 dB in the weak segment and 14.2 dB in the strong one.
The step grows on its own when the signal becomes strong and shrinks when it becomes weak.
same sign, \(+1\): overload, \(\times K\)
opposite signs, \(-1\): granular, \(\div K\)

SQNR \(= 10\log_{10}(P_X/D)\); for PCM, \(10\log_{10}(0.1384/0.0046) = 14.8\) dB.

\(e_n = \hat{x}_n - x_n\) is the error column of each table.
PCM spends more bits and errs more: it does not use the correlation between samples and wastes the range, with 2 of 8 levels used.
DPCM only quantizes jumps of up to about 0.1: with \(\Delta = 0.06\), four times smaller than that of PCM, it gives almost 10 dB more with a third fewer bits.
DM, with one bit, still beats PCM: the signal is slow and changes by about \(\Delta\) per sample.
Caution: the result depends on the signal. For a fast signal, the jumps would exceed 0.09 and 0.1; DPCM and DM would go into overload and PCM, which does not look at the past, would win.

Uniform PCM is the reference: 6 dB per bit and an SQNR tied to loudness.
Companding makes the SQNR flat with the same 8 bits.
DPCM replaces the sample by the jump and saves bits when \(\rho\) is high.
DM takes the idea to the extreme, with 1 bit and fast sampling.
In the range of 20 to 60 kbit/s, adaptive DM with \(K = 1.5\) is 5 to 10 dB above fixed-step DM for speech.
They all copy the waveform without knowing anything about how it was produced.
The next idea is the opposite: model the source and send only the model parameters, the vocoders.
