Fourier series: the trigonometric form

Periodic signal, discrete frequencies · press → to advance
\(f(t)\) periodic, period \(T_0\), \(\omega_0 = 2\pi/T_0\)
\[ f(t) = \cssId{tg-a0}{a_0} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big[\cssId{tg-an}{a_n}\cos\big(\cssId{tg-nw}{n\omega_0}\,t\big) + b_n\sin\big(n\omega_0 t\big)\Big] \tag{1.1}\]
A periodic signal contains only discrete frequencies.
\(T_0,\ \omega_0\)
period; fundamental frequency, in rad/s
\(a_0\)
mean value of \(f(t)\) over one period
\(a_n,\ b_n\)

Euler enters the series

Sines and cosines become exponentials · press → to advance
Two identities turn sines and cosines into exponentials
\[ \cssId{eu-c}{\cos\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}}{2}} \qquad \cssId{eu-s}{\sin\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}}{2j}} \tag{1.2}\]
We apply both to the term of order \(n\) of the series
\[ \cssId{eu-ta}{a_n\cos(n\omega_0 t)} + \cssId{eu-tb}{b_n\sin(n\omega_0 t)} \]
Result, with \(\theta = n\omega_0 t\)
\[ = {\color{#2e7d32}\frac{a_n}{2}}\big(e^{jn\omega_0 t}+e^{-jn\omega_0 t}\big) + {\color{#c76a00}\frac{b_n}{2j}}\big(e^{jn\omega_0 t}-e^{-jn\omega_0 t}\big) \]
\(\theta,\ j\)
angle, in rad; imaginary unit, \(j^2=-1\)
\(n\)
order of the harmonic, a positive integer
use the cosine identity, with \(\theta = n\omega_0 t\)
use the sine identity

Grouping the exponentials

The exponential form of the series · press → to advance
Recalling that \(1/j = -j\) and collecting the terms with the same exponential
\[ \cssId{ag-a}{\frac{a_n - j b_n}{2}}\, e^{jn\omega_0 t} + \cssId{ag-b}{\frac{a_n + j b_n}{2}}\, e^{-jn\omega_0 t} \]
Summing over all \(n\), positive and negative:
Exponential series
\[ \boxed{\;f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\, e^{jn\omega_0 t}\;} \tag{1.3}\]
\(c_n\)
complex coefficient of the exponential \(e^{jn\omega_0 t}\), \(c_0 = a_0\)
\(c_n^{*}\)
complex conjugate of \(c_n\)
call it \(c_n\)
it is the conjugate, \(c_{-n} = c_n^{*}\)

From the exponential to the polar form

A conjugate pair is a real cosine · press → to advance
Complex coefficient in magnitude and phase
\[ c_n = |c_n|\, e^{j\theta_n}, \qquad c_{-n} = |c_n|\, e^{-j\theta_n} \]
The conjugate pair recombines into a single real cosine:
Sum of the pair, by identity (1.2)
\[ c_n e^{jn\omega_0 t} + c_{-n} e^{-jn\omega_0 t} = \cssId{po-r}{2|c_n|\cos\big(n\omega_0 t + \theta_n\big)} \tag{1.4}\]
The sum of a conjugate pair of exponentials is always a real cosine.
\(|c_n|,\ \theta_n\)
magnitude and phase of \(c_n\), \(\theta_n = \arg c_n\)
\(|c_n|\big[e^{j(n\omega_0 t+\theta_n)} + e^{-j(n\omega_0 t+\theta_n)}\big]\) and Euler with \(\theta = n\omega_0 t + \theta_n\)

The polar form of the series

Amplitude and phase of each harmonic · press → to advance
Summing the conjugate pairs (1.4) from \(n=1\) to infinity
\[ \boxed{\;f(t) = C_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \cssId{pp-a}{C_n}\cos\big(n\omega_0 t + \cssId{pp-b}{\theta_n}\big)\;} \tag{1.5}\]
Each harmonic is described by an amplitude and a phase.
These are exactly the two pieces of information carried by the magnitude spectrum and the phase spectrum.

mean value, \(C_0 = c_0 = a_0\)
\(C_n\)

Isolating a coefficient: multiply

How to find \(c_k\) from \(f(t)\) · press → to advance
We start from the series (1.3)
\[ f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\, e^{jn\omega_0 t} \]
We multiply both sides by \(e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\), with integer \(k\)
\[ f(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\, \cssId{mu-a}{e^{jn\omega_0 t}\, e^{-jk\omega_0 t}} \]
\(k\)
index of the coefficient we want to isolate
same base: the exponents add up, \(e^{j(n-k)\omega_0 t}\)

Isolating a coefficient: integrate

One whole period · press → to advance
Adding the exponents
\[ f(t)\,e^{-jk\omega_0 t} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\, e^{j(n-k)\omega_0 t} \]
Integrating both sides over one period
\[ \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} f(t)\, e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\, dt = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n \cssId{or-a}{\int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} e^{j(n-k)\omega_0 t}\, dt} \tag{1.6}\]
Let \(m = n-k\) and solve the two possible cases.
everything depends on this integral. What is its value?

Solving the integral: case \(m = n-k \neq 0\)

Antiderivative of the exponential · press → to advance
The integral in (1.6) with \(m = n-k\)
\[ I_m = \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} e^{jm\omega_0 t}\, dt \tag{1.7}\]
Antiderivative of \(e^{jm\omega_0 t}\), valid for \(m\neq 0\)
\[ = \left[\frac{e^{jm\omega_0 t}}{jm\omega_0}\right]_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} \]
Evaluating at both limits
\[ = \frac{1}{jm\omega_0}\Big(e^{\,jm\,\cssId{in-b}{\omega_0 T_0/2}} - e^{-jm\,\omega_0 T_0/2}\Big) \]
\(m,\ I_m\)
\(m = n-k\), an integer; value of the integral
\(\omega_0 T_0/2 = \pi\), since \(\omega_0 = 2\pi/T_0\)

Solving the integral: the result is zero

Different harmonics cancel out · press → to advance
By Euler's identity, the difference in the numerator is a sine
\[ I_m = \frac{e^{jm\pi} - e^{-jm\pi}}{jm\omega_0} = \frac{2j\,\cssId{zn-a}{\sin(m\pi)}}{jm\omega_0} \]
Therefore
\[ \boxed{\;I_m = 0 \quad \text{for every } m \neq 0\;} \tag{1.8}\]
Exponentials of different harmonics cancel out over one period.
\(m\) is an integer, so \(\sin(m\pi) = 0\)

Solving the integral: case \(m = 0\)

When \(n = k\) the exponent vanishes · press → to advance
When \(n = k\) the exponent vanishes
\[ I_0 = \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} \cssId{i0-a}{e^{\,j\cdot 0\cdot \omega_0 t}}\, dt \]
Integral of \(1\) over one period
\[ = \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} dt = \frac{T_0}{2} - \left(-\frac{T_0}{2}\right) = T_0 \]
equals \(1\) for every \(t\)

The sum collapses

Orthogonality · press → to advance
The two cases, (1.8) and \(I_0 = T_0\)
\[ \boxed{\;I_{n-k} = \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} e^{j(n-k)\omega_0 t}\, dt = \begin{cases} T_0, & n = k \\[2pt] 0, & n \neq k \end{cases}\;} \tag{1.9}\]
Back to the sum (1.6)
\[ \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} f(t)\, e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\, dt = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\, \underbrace{\cssId{cp-a}{I_{n-k}}}_{\text{zero, except } n=k} \]
Orthogonality lets us isolate one coefficient at a time.
only \(c_k\,T_0\) remains

The series coefficients

Analysis and synthesis · press → to advance
Only the term \(n=k\) remains
\[ \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} f(t)\, e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\, dt = c_k\, T_0 \]
Isolating the coefficient
\[ \boxed{\;c_k = \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} f(t)\, e^{-jk\omega_0 t}\, dt\;} \tag{1.10}\]
Analysis is projecting the signal onto each exponential.
Synthesis is adding these projections back, with weights \(c_k\).

Derivation of the Fourier transform
Letting \(T_0\to\infty\), the series sum becomes an integral and the envelope becomes the continuous spectrum \(F(\omega)\).

Step 1: put \(c_n\) inside the series

Derivation · press → to advance
The two series equations, (1.3) and (1.10)
\[ \begin{aligned} f(t) &= \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n e^{jn\omega_0 t} \\ c_n &= \frac{1}{T_0}\int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} f(\tau)\, e^{-jn\omega_0 \tau}\, d\tau \end{aligned} \tag{2.1}\]
Substituting the second into the first:
\(\tau\) is the dummy variable of the integral
\[ f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} {\color{#2e7d32}\frac{1}{T_0}} \left[\;\cssId{p1-a}{\int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} f(\tau)\, e^{-jn\omega_0 \tau}\, d\tau}\;\right] e^{jn\omega_0 t} \]
The bracket is the natural candidate for a continuous spectrum.
depends on \(n\) only through \(n\omega_0\)

Step 2: name the envelope \(F(n\omega_0)\)

Derivation · press → to advance
We give a name to the bracket of the previous step:
Definition, comparing with (2.1)
\[ \boxed{\;F(n\omega_0) \triangleq \int_{-T_0/2}^{T_0/2} f(\tau)\, e^{-jn\omega_0 \tau}\, d\tau = \cssId{p2-a}{T_0\, c_n}\;} \tag{2.2}\]
Thus \(c_n = F(n\omega_0)/T_0\), and the synthesis series becomes:
Plug \(c_n\) into (1.3)
\[ f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{F(n\omega_0)}{T_0}\, e^{jn\omega_0 t} \tag{2.3}\]
\(F(n\omega_0)\)
envelope evaluated at harmonic \(n\)
it is the dashed envelope of the growing-period slide

Step 2b: make the spacing appear

Derivation · press → to advance
The factor \(1/T_0\) hides the spacing between harmonics. Since \(\omega_0 = 2\pi/T_0\)
\[ \frac{1}{T_0} = \frac{\omega_0}{2\pi} \]
Substituting into (2.3)
\[ f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} F(n\omega_0)\, e^{jn\omega_0 t}\; {\color{#7b2d8e}\omega_0} \tag{2.4}\]
The highlighted term is the distance between two neighboring frequency components. Keep this in mind for the next step.

Step 3: recognize a Riemann sum

Height times base · press → to advance
Call the spacing between harmonics \(\Delta\omega = \omega_0\):
(2.4) with \(\omega_0 = \Delta\omega\)
\[ f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{n} \underbrace{F(n\omega_0)e^{jn\omega_0 t}}_{\text{height}} \;\underbrace{\Delta\omega}_{\text{base}} \tag{2.5}\]
Each term of the sum is the area of a rectangle under the curve \(F(\omega)e^{j\omega t}\).
Ever thinner rectangles converge to the integral.

Step 4: the limit \(T_0 \to \infty\)

Three simultaneous substitutions · press → to advance
Since \(\omega_0 = 2\pi/T_0\), stretching the period squeezes the spacing. Three simultaneous substitutions:
The integration limits \([-T_0/2,\,T_0/2]\) also open up to the whole real line.

Fourier transform pair

Analysis and synthesis · press → to advance
Analysis: the bracket (2.2) with \(T_0\to\infty\) and \(n\omega_0\to\omega\)
\[ {\color{#2e7d32}F(\omega)} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\, e^{-j\omega t}\, dt \tag{2.6}\]
Synthesis: the sum (2.5) becomes an integral
\[ {\color{#7b2d8e}f(t)} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega)\, e^{j\omega t}\, d\omega \tag{2.7}\]
Compact notation: \(f(t) \ftp F(\omega)\).

The two differences between there and back

Comparing (2.6) and (2.7) · press → to advance
Analysis and synthesis, (2.6) and (2.7)
\[ \begin{aligned} F(\omega) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\, e^{\cssId{pt-a}{-j\omega t}}\, dt \\[10pt] f(t) &= \cssId{pt-c}{\frac{1}{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega)\, e^{\cssId{pt-b}{+j\omega t}}\, d\omega \end{aligned} \]
The series adds discrete frequency components; the transform integrates a continuum of frequencies.
exponents with opposite signs
\(1/2\pi\) only on the way back, because \(\omega\) is in rad/s

Interpreting the Fourier transform

\(F(\omega)\) is a complex number for each \(\omega\) · press →
For each frequency \(\omega\), the value \(F(\omega)\) is a complex number
\[ F(\omega) = \cssId{it-a}{|F(\omega)|}\; e^{\,j\,\cssId{it-b}{\phi(\omega)}} \tag{2.8}\]
Magnitude and phase together describe \(f(t)\) completely.
\(|F(\omega)|\)
magnitude spectrum
\(\phi(\omega)\)
phase spectrum, in rad
magnitude spectrum: the size of the component
phase spectrum: the shift of the component

Magnitude and phase, side by side

What each half of the spectrum tells us · press → to advance
The magnitude tells how much of each frequency there is in \(f(t)\).
The phase tells where each component starts, that is, how much it is shifted in time.
Discarding the phase and keeping only the magnitude destroys the signal.
3
Magnitude and phase spectra
Magnitude and phase, or real and imaginary parts: two ways of writing the same \(F(\omega)\).

Spectral representation

Polar form and rectangular form · press → to advance
The transform is generally complex
\[ F(\omega) = |F(\omega)| e^{j\phi(\omega)} = \Real\{F(\omega)\} + j\Imag\{F(\omega)\} \tag{3.1}\]
Magnitude spectrum: hypotenuse of the triangle
\[ |F(\omega)| = \sqrt{\Real^2\{F(\omega)\} + \Imag^2\{F(\omega)\}} \tag{3.2}\]
Phase spectrum: angle of the triangle
\[ \phi(\omega) = \arctan\left(\frac{\Imag\{F(\omega)\}}{\Real\{F(\omega)\}}\right) \tag{3.3}\]
The complete spectrum requires both pieces of information, magnitude and phase.
For real signals, useful symmetries hold: \(|F(-\omega)| = |F(\omega)|\), even magnitude; \(\phi(-\omega) = -\phi(\omega)\), odd phase.
\(\Real,\ \Imag\)
real part and imaginary part

Examples
Causal decaying exponential and two-sided exponential pulse, with the integrals solved on the board.

Example 1: decaying exponential

Setting up the integral · press → to advance
Causal exponential signal
\[ f(t) = \begin{cases} e^{-at} & t \geq 0 \\ 0 & t \lt 0 \end{cases} = e^{-at} u(t), \quad a \gt 0 \tag{4.1}\]
Definition (2.6); \(u(t)\) zeroes \(t\lt 0\)
\[ F(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t) e^{-j\omega t} dt = \int_{\cssId{e1-0}{0}}^{\infty} e^{-at} e^{-j\omega t} dt \]
Combine the exponents and integrate
\[ = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(a+j\omega)t} dt = \left[ \frac{e^{-(a+j\omega)t}}{-(a+j\omega)} \right]_{0}^{\infty} \]
\(a,\ u(t)\)
decay constant, in 1/s; unit step
before \(t=0\) the signal is zero

Example 1: decaying exponential, continued

Evaluating and rationalizing · press → to advance
Since \(a \gt 0\), \(e^{-(a+j\omega)t} \to 0\) as \(t \to \infty\)
\[ F(\omega) = \cssId{e1-z}{0} - \frac{1}{-(a+j\omega)} = \frac{1}{a+j\omega} \tag{4.2}\]
Rationalizing with the conjugate
\[ F(\omega) = \frac{1}{a+j\omega} \cdot \frac{a-j\omega}{a-j\omega} = \frac{a-j\omega}{\cssId{e1-d}{a^2+\omega^2}} \]
\(|e^{-(a+j\omega)t}| = e^{-at} \to 0\)
\((a+j\omega)(a-j\omega) = a^2 + \omega^2\)

Example 1: final result

Real part, imaginary part, magnitude and phase · press → to advance
Separating real and imaginary parts
\[ \boxed{F(\omega) = \frac{a}{a^2+\omega^2} - j\frac{\omega}{a^2+\omega^2}} \tag{4.3}\]
Magnitude spectrum, by (3.2)
\[ |F(\omega)| = \cssId{e1-m}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2+\omega^2}}} \tag{4.4}\]
Phase spectrum, by (3.3)
\[ \phi(\omega) = \cssId{e1-f}{-\arctan\!\left(\frac{\omega}{a}\right)} \tag{4.5}\]
\(\sqrt{\dfrac{a^2+\omega^2}{(a^2+\omega^2)^2}}\)
\(\arctan\dfrac{-\omega}{a}\), and the arctangent is odd

Example 1: analyzing the result

Peak, 3 dB points and bandwidth · press → to advance
Transform pair
\[ \boxed{f(t) = e^{-at}u(t) \;\ftp\; F(\omega) = \frac{1}{a+j\omega}} \tag{4.6}\]
Magnitude \(|F(\omega)| = 1/\sqrt{a^2+\omega^2}\), with a Lorentzian shape.
Phase \(\phi(\omega) = -\arctan(\omega/a)\).
At \(\omega = 0\) the value is maximum: \(|F(0)| = 1/\sqrt{a^2} = 1/a\).
At \(\omega = \pm a\) there is a 3 dB drop: \(|F(\pm a)| = 1/\sqrt{2a^2} = 1/(a\sqrt{2})\), and \(20\log_{10}(1/\sqrt2) = -3.01\) dB.
Bandwidth \(B \approx a\), tied to the decay constant.
Fast decay in time produces a wide spectrum in frequency.

Two known halves · press → to advance
Symmetric two-sided exponential pulse

This signal can be decomposed into

Each part already has a known transform from Example 1.
holds for \(t \gt 0\)

Example 2: computing the transform

Splitting the integral into two intervals · press → to advance
Definition (2.6) over two intervals
\[ F(\omega) = \underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{0} e^{at} e^{-j\omega t} dt}_{t \lt 0} + \underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-at} e^{-j\omega t} dt}_{t \gt 0} \]
For \(t \lt 0\)
\[ \int_{-\infty}^{0} e^{(a-j\omega)t} dt = \cssId{e2-n}{\left[\frac{e^{(a-j\omega)t}}{a-j\omega}\right]_{-\infty}^{0}} = \frac{1}{a-j\omega} \]
For \(t \gt 0\), it is the calculation (4.2)
\[ \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(a+j\omega)t} dt = \frac{1}{a+j\omega} \]
at \(0\) it equals \(1/(a-j\omega)\); at \(-\infty\), \(e^{at}\to 0\)

Example 2: result

Real and even spectrum · press → to advance
Sum of the two parts, common denominator
\[ F(\omega) = \frac{1}{a-j\omega} + \frac{1}{a+j\omega} = \frac{(a+j\omega) + (a-j\omega)}{(a-j\omega)(a+j\omega)} \]
Numerator \(2a\), denominator \(a^2+\omega^2\)
\[ \boxed{F(\omega) = \frac{2a}{a^2+\omega^2}} \tag{4.8}\]
\(F(\omega)\) is real, with zero phase, because \(f(t)\) is even.
Lorentzian spectrum centered at \(\omega = 0\).
\(F(0) = 2a/a^2 = 2/a\) and \(F(\pm a) = 2a/(2a^2) = 1/a\).

5
Time-frequency uncertainty
Squeezing a signal in time widens its spectrum. The product of the two widths has a floor.

The time-frequency uncertainty principle

Squeeze the signal in time and see what happens to the spectrum · press →
The product of the two widths has a floor
\[ \Delta t \cdot \Delta \omega \geq \tfrac{1}{2} \tag{5.1}\]

The Heisenberg-Gabor inequality, with \(\Delta t\) and \(\Delta\omega\) measured as standard deviations of \(|f(t)|^2\) and \(|F(\omega)|^2\).

Time-frequency uncertainty: consequences

Beamer figure · press → to advance
Practical consequences:
Short pulses require a large bandwidth.
Narrowband signals need a long duration.
A fundamental trade-off in communication systems.
The Gaussian reaches the minimum bound \(\Delta t \cdot \Delta\omega = \frac{1}{2}\).
